What a coastline remembers

The tide had receded further than the fishermen could recall, exposing a latticework of tidal channels that shimmered under an indifferent sky. Marisol walked out across the flats, her boots sinking into sediment that had been accumulating since before the village existed. Every few paces she stopped to scrutinize something the water had abandoned — a vertebra, a shard of blue glass worn opaque, the desiccated husk of a crab.

Her grandmother had been adamant that the sea was not indifferent at all, merely inscrutable, and that the distinction mattered enormously. It was a proposition Marisol had spent two decades trying to corroborate and had never quite managed to refute either. The old woman's convictions were impervious to argument; she simply outlasted her interlocutors.

The archive beneath

What the flats preserved was not history so much as its residue. Sediment accretes in strata, and each stratum is a ledger of what the water was carrying that year — ash from a distant conflagration, microplastics, the pollen of crops long since supplanted. Geologists call this the stratigraphic record, but Marisol preferred her grandmother's word for it: the remembering.

She had read that anthropogenic sediment now constitutes a measurable fraction of what accumulates on continental shelves, and that this fraction is increasing at a rate which some researchers find alarming and others consider merely notable. The disparity in interpretation was itself instructive. Two people can look at the same core sample and see either an admonition or a curiosity.

Consider the humble oyster, whose shell is an inadvertent instrument of great precision. It lays down calcium carbonate in daily increments, and the width of each increment is contingent on the temperature and salinity of the surrounding water. A shell is therefore a transcript. Break one open and you can reconstruct, with unnerving fidelity, the weather of a summer nobody wrote down.
